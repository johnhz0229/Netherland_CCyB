# Storyline (v3, 2026-10-01): "The Promise of March 2020"

**Title:** *Refilling the Buffer: Why the Netherlands Raised its CCyB When the Rulebook Said Zero*

**The story in one breath.**
- In March 2020, COVID hit. Sweden, Denmark, France and others released their countercyclical buffers within days. The Netherlands had nothing to release.
- DNB improvised: it cut the big banks' structural buffer and made a promise. Once things were back to normal, it would rebuild that capital as a 2% CCyB.
- In 2022 and 2023 DNB kept the promise, even though the official Basel indicator pointed to 0% and inflation and rate hikes were hitting the economy.
- Our investigation asks whether this was a tightening against an overheating credit boom. The evidence says no. It was the refilling of a releasable buffer, largely offset by cuts elsewhere. It cost banks little, and lending showed no sign of contraction.

**Thesis.** *Normalisation, not tightening.* Measured against the pre-COVID baseline, DNB re-composed bank capital: less structural, more releasable. It did not respond to a credit cycle. (Measured against 2021, it was a modest increase, and we say so.)

**Narrative devices**
- **Motif:** "the promise of March 2020". It opens the talk (slide 2), drives the puzzle (slide 8) and closes it (slide 21).
- **Structure:** a detective case. Puzzle → two suspects (overheating vs normalisation) → evidence → verdict → consequences.
- **One rule for every slide:** the title states the conclusion; the body proves it; the last spoken sentence hands over to the next slide.

**Timing (≈25 min).**
- Part I: Why buffers must be releasable, slides 2–7, ≈7 min.
- Part II: The Dutch case, slides 8–15, ≈9 min.
- Part III: Consequences, slides 16–20, ≈6.5 min.
- Conclusion: slide 21, ≈2 min.
- Slide 1 is the stand-alone one-pager, shown for ≈0.5 min at the start.

**Proposed split, more balanced than "Part I + conclusion vs Part II".**
- Speaker A: slides 1–7 and 16–21 (≈15 min).
- Speaker B: slides 8–15, the investigation (≈10 min).
- Hand-overs at 7→8 ("…and that is where our puzzle begins") and 15→16 ("…so what did it cost?").
- B carries the analytical core (slide 14) from puzzle to verdict without interruption.

## Slide 1 · One-pager (must work stand-alone)

**Title:** *The Netherlands raised its CCyB to 2% when the Basel rule said 0%. It was refilling a buffer, not fighting a boom.*

- **The puzzle.**
  - DNB raised the CCyB from 0% to 1% (May 2022) and then to 2% (May 2023).
  - The Basel credit-to-GDP gap stood at −33pp and −47pp, so the buffer guide implied 0% both times. These are the latest data available at each decision: 2021Q4 and 2022Q4.
- **Environment.**
  - The economy had recovered from COVID.
  - Inflation was 11.6% (HICP 2022) and ECB rates were rising.
  - House prices first boomed (+19% y/y), then fell.
  - Banks were well capitalised (CET1 17.7%).
- **Criteria and risks.**
  - DNB's 2022 framework sets 2% as the level for a "standard risk environment". It is calibrated on past crisis losses, not on credit growth.
  - The target is unforeseeable shocks. It also keeps the March 2020 promise to rebuild releasable capital.
- **Implications.**
  - **Cheap:** at most about one fifth of the large banks' CET1 headroom.
  - **Largely a swap:** structural buffers were cut in 2020 and 2024.
  - **No sign of contraction:** Dutch lending rates moved with euro-area peers, and household credit grew faster.
  - **€6.7bn of releasable capital** now exists.
- **Trade-offs.**
  - The policy relies on discretion and credibility.
  - The release mechanism is untested.
  - Its importance grows when the mortgage risk-weight floor expires (30 Nov 2026).

## PART I · Why buffers must be releasable (Speaker A, ≈7 min)

### Slide 2 · Hook: March 2020
**Title:** *When COVID hit, other countries released their buffers; the Netherlands had none to release.*
- **Visual:** a "COVID week" strip showing CCyB releases in March–April 2020:
  - Denmark (12 Mar), Norway (to 1%, 13 Mar), Sweden (16 Mar), Lithuania and Germany (31 Mar), France and Ireland (1 Apr)
  - NL: 0%, nothing to release
- **Story beat:**
  - DNB improvised. On 17 Mar 2020 it cut the systemic buffers of ING, Rabobank and ABN AMRO (3% → 2.5/2/1.5%) and postponed the mortgage risk-weight floor, freeing €8bn of capital.
  - It also made a promise (quote on slide): *"Once the situation is back to normal, DNB will compensate the systemic buffers reduction by gradually increasing the countercyclical capital buffer to 2%."*
- **Last line:** "To understand why that promise matters, we need to understand why some capital has to be releasable at all."
- **Sources:** ESRB CCyB table ✔; DNB press release 17 Mar 2020 ✔. (UK omitted: not in our verified sources.)

### Slide 3 · Why releasable capital matters
**Title:** *Bank capital amplifies the cycle, and banks won't use buffers that are not explicitly released.*
- **Mechanism:**
  - In a downturn, losses eat capital.
  - To keep their ratios, banks shrink lending.
  - The credit crunch then deepens the recession.
- **The COVID evidence:**
  - Banks close to their buffer thresholds cut lending, even though they were *allowed* to use the buffers (Couaillier et al. 2022, ECB WP 2644) ✔.
  - The reason is stigma and distribution restrictions (MDA).
  - Only a buffer the authority formally releases is truly usable.
- **Last line:** "That is the job of one specific buffer."

### Slide 4 · CCyB design and the capital stack
**Title:** *The CCyB is the only layer of the capital stack built to be switched off.*
- **Visual (new figure):** the capital stack, from P1 4.5% up through P2R, CCoB 2.5%, O-SII/SRB and the CCyB on top. Releasable layers are highlighted, and the MDA line is marked.
- **Design:**
  - 0–2.5% of risk-weighted assets (RWA) on domestic exposures.
  - Increases take effect after 12 months; releases take effect immediately.
  - Mandatory EU reciprocity, so foreign banks lending in NL also hold it.
- **Last line:** "The question is when to switch it on, and Basel gave a rule for that."

### Slide 5 · The original rule
**Title:** *Basel's rule: switch the buffer on when credit grows faster than its trend.*
- **The rule:**
  - Credit-to-GDP gap = actual ratio − HP-filter trend (λ = 400,000).
  - Gap < 2pp → 0%; gap > 10pp → 2.5%; linear in between.
- **Basis:** Drehmann et al. (2010) ✔, Drehmann & Juselius (2014) ✔.
- **Last line:** "Elegant on paper. Let's test it on Dutch data."

### Slide 6 · The rule fails on Dutch data
**Title:** *On Dutch data, the credit gap missed the crisis, raised a false alarm, and was rewritten after the fact.*
- **Visual:** Fig. 1. Our replication matches the BIS gap to within 0.00005pp over 1971–2026 ✔.
- **Three failures:**
  1. **Missed:** −13pp in 2007Q4, on the eve of the GFC.
  2. **False alarm:** +17pp in 2012, during a house-price bust and recession. Falling GDP inflated the ratio (Repullo & Saurina 2011 ✔).
  3. **Revised:** 2016Q1 read −0.6pp in real time but +26.9pp ex post (Edge & Meisenzahl 2011 ✔).
- **Last line:** "COVID added a fourth lesson: a buffer that is empty when the shock arrives is useless."

### Slide 7 · The fix: a positive neutral CCyB
**Title:** *Many authorities now keep the buffer filled in normal times, ready for shocks that no indicator predicts.*
- **Adoption:**
  - BCBS (2024) lists 17 jurisdictions with a positive neutral rate.
  - The Netherlands, Sweden, the UK and Poland target 2% ✔.
- **Calibration:** historical losses, stress tests and models. The ECB losses-to-buffer approach gives 1.1–1.8% (De Nora et al. 2025) ✔.
- **Same trick as NL:** the UK offset its 2% by lowering Pillar 2A and resolution requirements ✔.
- **Hand-over line:** "The Netherlands adopted this approach in 2022, and that is where our puzzle begins."

## PART II · The Dutch case (Speaker B, ≈9 min)

### Slide 8 · The puzzle
**Title:** *The rulebook said 0%. DNB went to 2%. Why?*
- **Visual:** two numbers side by side.
  - Official gap at the decisions: −33pp (data to 2021Q4) and −47pp (data to 2022Q4), so the buffer guide gives 0%.
  - DNB: 1%, then 2%.
- **DNB admits it** (17 Sep 2026 ✔): 2% is *"above the 0% implied by the Basel buffer guide."*
- **Two suspects:**
  - **H1 Overheating:** DNB saw a credit or housing boom the gap missed, and tightened.
  - **H2 Normalisation:** DNB refilled a releasable buffer as promised, regardless of the cycle.
- **Last line:** "Let's look at the scene of the decision."

### Slide 9 · Timeline
**Title:** *The 2% was promised in 2020, two years before it was set.*
- **Visual (new figure):** a timeline.
  - 17 Mar 2020: structural buffer cut + promise
  - 29 Dec 2020: SRB converted to O-SII buffers
  - 1 Jan 2022: mortgage risk-weight floor in force
  - Feb 2022: new framework
  - 25 May 2022: 1% announced
  - 31 May 2023: 2% announced + O-SII cuts announced
  - 31 May 2024: both binding
  - 2024–Sep 2026: held at 2%
  - 30 Nov 2026: floor expires
- **Last line:** "The path was announced in advance. What did the economy look like when DNB walked it?"
- **Sources:** DNB releases ✔, ESRB notifications ✔.

### Slide 10 · Environment at the decisions
**Title:** *DNB raised the buffer in a recovered, inflationary economy with strong banks.*
- **Economy:**
  - GDP back above its pre-COVID level in 2021Q3 ✔.
  - HICP inflation 11.6% in 2022 ✔.
  - ECB's first hike on 21 Jul 2022 (+50bp) ✔.
- **Housing:** prices +19% y/y in 2022Q1, then down to −4% y/y by 2023Q2 ✔.
- **Banks:**
  - CET1 17.7% at end-2021, above the EU average of 15.7% ✔.
  - Net interest income +6.7% in 2022 ✔.
- **Last line:** "Raising a buffer in an inflation shock sounds risky. So what did DNB say it was doing?"

### Slide 11 · DNB's framework and stated reasons
**Title:** *DNB's own framework calls 2% the "normality" setting, and its stated reasons were not about credit growth.*
- **Framework (2022) ✔:**
  - Four phases: recovery → normality (2%) → increased risk (>2%) → materialisation (release).
  - Build-up of "1% per year … to reach the neutral level of 2% after two years".
  - Calibration: peak losses of €12bn (2007–16) → 2% ≈ €6bn releasable → up to €150bn of lending.
  - "Also taking into account the impact of the buffer reduction in March 2020."
- **2022 reasons ✔:** recovery from COVID; risks "normal to elevated"; banks well capitalised; "not expected to lead to higher lending rates".
- **2023 reasons ✔:** "the credit-to-GDP gap shows no signs of excessive credit growth"; house prices falling; investors' risk appetite and debt sustainability.
- **Last line:** "Words are cheap. Let's check the data, starting with credit."

### Slide 12 · Evidence 1: credit was cold
**Title:** *Every credit indicator was cooling.*
- **Credit gap:** −33pp and −47pp at the decisions, at the bottom of its 2005–19 range.
- **Debt-service ratio (households):** 14.6% in 2022Q1, the lowest since 2005 ✔.
- **Household debt/GDP:** fell from 110% (2019Q4) to 107% (2022Q1) and 100% (2023Q1) ✔.
- **Honest footnote:** alternative trend filters are noisy around COVID. The two-sided HP filter (+4.2pp) and the Hamilton filter read slightly positive in 2021Q4, because the 2020 GDP collapse and rebound distort trends. This reinforces slide 6: none of these is a safe trigger.
- **Last line:** "If H1 is right, the boom must be somewhere else, and there is one place to look."

### Slide 13 · Evidence 2: housing, the strongest counter-evidence
**Title:** *Housing was the one hot spot, but DNB kept raising after it cooled.*
- **Visual:** Fig. 2 (house prices, debt, debt-service ratio).
- **Hot:**
  - Nominal house prices +19% y/y in 2022Q1 ✔.
  - Real prices 16% above the 2007 peak ✔.
  - Household debt/GDP 107%, among the world's highest and almost twice the euro area's 57.7% ✔.
  - The ratio fell only because GDP grew; the debt stock rose 12% (2019Q4–2023Q1) ✔.
- **But:**
  - Real house prices fell about 9% from 2022Q2 to 2023Q2 ✔.
  - DNB raised to 2% *in* that downturn.
- **Last line:** "So how do the two suspects stack up against all the evidence?"

### Slide 14 · The verdict (analytical core, the clearest slide)
**Title:** *Every prediction fits normalisation, none fits overheating.*

| If DNB were… | Normalising | Fighting overheating | What happened |
|---|---|---|---|
| Link to credit indicators | none | rises with them | gap, debt-service ratio and household debt ratio falling; CCyB up ✔ |
| Path | +1pp a year, stop at 2% | follows risk | 1% → 2% → held ✔ |
| 2023: house prices falling, rates jumping | raise anyway | pause | raised ✔ |
| 2024–26: house prices rising again (+10% y/y, Mar-2026) | stay at 2% | go above 2% (phase 3) | held at 2% ✔ |
| Other buffers | cut structural buffers to compensate | keep them | cut in 2020 and 2024 ✔ |

- **Spoken:** "Five predictions, five matches. The one fact that fits overheating, house prices in early 2022, is handled by a different tool, which brings us to the last piece of evidence."

### Slide 15 · Evidence 3: a swap, with each risk getting its own tool
**Title:** *DNB moved capital from structural to releasable buffers, and gave each risk its own instrument.*
- **The swap:**
  - 2020: SRB cut by 0.5–1.5pp.
  - 2024: O-SII cut by 0.25–0.75pp (worth about €2.7bn, own calculation).
  - Against this: CCyB +€6.7bn.
  - Net 2023 step ≈ +€0.7bn.
- **DNB's own words:**
  - 2020: "more or less capital-neutral".
  - 2023: "limited increase in the net capital requirements" (Knot, 7 Jun 2023) ✔.
  - FSR 2023: "slightly increases" ✔.
- **Tools:**
  - housing → borrower-based limits + mortgage risk-weight floor
  - size and systemic importance → O-SII (banking sector 400% → 280% of GDP) ✔
  - unforeseen shocks → CCyB
- **Hand-over line:** "Verdict: normalisation. But a policy can be well-intended and still costly. So what did it cost?"

## PART III · Consequences (Speaker A, ≈6.5 min)

### Slide 16 · Implication 1: cheap for banks
**Title:** *The full 2% absorbs at most a fifth of the big banks' spare capital.*
- **Visual:** a bar chart by bank (ING, Rabobank, ABN AMRO, de Volksbank) comparing CET1 headroom above requirement with the CCyB.
- **Numbers:**
  - Headroom: €42bn at end-2021 and €36bn at end-2022.
  - The CCyB is ≈8% and ≈19% of it (upper bounds) ✔.
  - Headroom shrank because of RWA growth and distributions, not the CCyB.
- **Context:** banks' profits rose with interest rates (net interest income +6.7%).
- **Last line:** "Cheap for banks, but did borrowers pay?"

### Slide 17 · Implication 2: no sign of contraction
**Title:** *Credit kept flowing, and its price moved with the rest of the euro area.*
- **Visual:** Fig. 3, the event study (NL vs clean controls AT, FI, IT, MT, LU, with a placebo band).
- **Rates:** Dutch lending rates stayed inside the placebo range at all three dates (May-22, May-23, May-24).
- **Volumes:**
  - Household credit grew faster than peers ✔.
  - Bank credit 2022Q1–2026Q1: NL +16.6% vs euro area +7.9%. But 2019–22: NL +4.3% vs +11.3%, so part of this is catch-up.
- **DNB (17 Sep 2026) ✔:** "no signs of constraints in bank credit supply".
- **Caveat on slide:** one treated country, contaminated controls, housing rebound. "No sign of contraction" is not a causal effect.
- **Last line:** "So the cost was small. What did the Netherlands get for it?"

### Slide 18 · Implication 3: what the buffer buys
**Title:** *Next time, DNB will have €6.7bn it can release overnight.*
- **The buffer:**
  - Releasable-by-design capital went from €0 to ≈€6.7bn, about half of the €12bn peak losses after the GFC.
  - Released, it could support up to €150bn of lending ✔.
- **Why it matters:**
  - Releases are powerful precisely in bad states (Lang & Menno 2023 ✔).
  - In normal times a 1pp rise cuts lending by about 0.1%; in bad times a 1pp release can raise it by up to about 10%.
- **Contrast:** 2020 relief had to be improvised by cutting structural buffers.
- **Last line:** "And the Netherlands is not alone."

### Slide 19 · Implication 4: a European shift
**Title:** *The Netherlands is part of a Europe-wide move to fill buffers early.*
- **Neighbours:**
  - France 1% (Jan 2024), Germany 0.75% (Feb 2023) ✔.
  - Belgium 1% (Oct 2024), Ireland 1.5% (Jun 2024) ✔.
  - Spain 1% (Oct 2026), Portugal 0.75% (Jan 2026) ✔ (ESRB).
- **Reciprocity:** foreign banks lending in NL hold the Dutch 2% too, which limits leakage (Aiyar et al. 2014 ✔).
- **Broader shift:** macroprudential policy is moving from mechanical rules to "guided discretion".
- **Last line:** "That shift has a price."

### Slide 20 · Critiques and open questions
**Title:** *Discretion buys flexibility, but it must earn credibility.*
- **Credibility:** without a rule, markets must trust DNB's judgement (rules vs discretion, Kydland & Prescott 1977 ✔).
- **Calibration:** why 2% and not 1.5%? The ECB range is 1.1–1.8%.
- **Never tested:** no release has happened yet. Will banks really lend the freed capital?
- **Baseline matters:** relative to 2021, it was a modest increase (+€3.3bn before the O-SII offset).
- **Stakes rise:** the mortgage floor expires 30 Nov 2026, leaving the CCyB as the main releasable buffer ✔.

## CONCLUSION (Speaker A, ≈2 min)

### Slide 21 · Back to March 2020
**Title:** *A promise kept: the buffer was refilled in calm times, for the next storm.*
- **Three answers to the assignment:**
  1. **Environment:** a recovered, inflationary economy with strong banks and cold credit.
  2. **Criteria and risks:** a normal-times level for unforeseeable shocks, rebuilding the releasable capital missing in 2020. Not a response to overheating.
  3. **Implications:** cheap, largely a swap, no sign of contraction, €6.7bn ready to release; the trade-off is reliance on discretion.
- **Closing line:** *"In March 2020 the Netherlands had nothing to release. Next time, it will. The best time to fill a buffer is when nobody thinks you need it."*

## Claims we deliberately do NOT make
- No causal claim on lending: only "no sign of contraction".
- Not "every gap measure says 0%": only the official Basel gap does at both decisions; alternative filters are noisy (slide 12 footnote).
- Not "zero releasable capital in 2020": €8bn was freed by improvisation, but none of it was releasable by design.
- Not "no tightening at all": relative to 2021 there was a modest net increase (slide 20).
- Nothing that rests only on search extracts (e.g. ING's 47bp CCyB).
