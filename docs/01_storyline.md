# Storyline (latest version: the normalisation thesis)

**Title:** Filling the Buffer in Calm Times: The Netherlands' CCyB Normalisation (2022–2023)

**One-line thesis:** COVID exposed the cost of an empty buffer. As the economy recovered, the Netherlands raised its CCyB from 0% to the 2% "normal-times" level, so that releasable capital would be ready for the next shock. The move was not a response to credit overheating, it cost banks little, and lending did not contract.

**Length:** about 25 minutes, 22 slides.
- Part I (what the CCyB is and how it evolved): about 7 minutes.
- Part II (Netherlands deep dive): about 15 minutes.
- Conclusion: about 3 minutes.

**Original assignment:** Analyse the economic and financial environment when the macroprudential authority raised the CCyB, the criteria supporting the decision and the financial-stability risks it addressed, and assess the wide-ranging implications.

---

## Slide 1 · One-pager (problem and conclusions)

- **Puzzle.** In 2022 and 2023, DNB raised the CCyB twice, to 2%. At the same time the Basel reference indicator, the credit-to-GDP gap, stood at −37pp and −55pp. The Basel rule would have set the buffer at 0%.
- **Answer 1 · Environment.**
  - Post-COVID recovery, high inflation and rate hikes.
  - House prices first boomed, then fell.
  - Banks were well capitalised and profitable.
  - The credit cycle was not heating up.
- **Answer 2 · Criteria and risks.**
  - DNB's 2022 framework defines 2% as the "normal-times" level.
  - The buffer targets unforeseeable shocks and the Dutch economy's structural vulnerabilities, not cyclical overheating.
- **Answer 3 · Implications.**
  - Low cost: the full 2% uses at most about a fifth of the four large banks' CET1 headroom (≈8% in 2021, ≈19% in 2022; bank disclosures).
  - Partly a swap: DNB cut systemic buffers in 2020 and 2023 and framed the CCyB as the replacement, so the net requirement barely rose.
  - No sign of a credit contraction.
  - About €6.7bn of releasable capital.
  - Trade-offs: more reliance on discretion, and the release mechanism has never been tested.
- Outlook: the mortgage risk-weight floor expires 30 Nov 2026, which makes the 2% CCyB the main releasable buffer.

## Part I: What the CCyB is and how it evolved

**Slide 2 · Lesson of the GFC: capital amplifies the cycle.**
- In booms, capital looks ample.
- In busts, banks deleverage to meet capital requirements, and the credit crunch deepens the recession.

**Slide 3 · CCyB design.**
- 0–2.5% of RWA, applied by location of exposure.
- Increases phase in over 12 months; releases take effect immediately.
- Mandatory EU reciprocity up to 2.5%.
- Figure: the capital stack, highlighting the CCyB as the only layer that can be released on demand.

**Slide 4 · The original rule: credit gap plus buffer guide.**
- A gap below 2pp maps to 0%; a gap above 10pp maps to 2.5%; linear in between.
- Basis: Drehmann et al. (2010) and Drehmann & Juselius (2014).

**Slide 5 · Problems with the rule, shown on Dutch data (Fig. 1).**
- Real-time vs ex-post revisions: in 2016Q1 the gap read −0.6pp in real time and +26.9pp ex post.
- Missed signal: −13pp in 2007, just before the GFC.
- False signal: +17pp in 2012, during a house-price bust and recession.
- Our replication of the BIS gap matches to within 0.0001pp.

**Slide 6 · COVID as a real-world stress test.**
- Countries with a positive CCyB (UK, Sweden, Norway, etc.) released it immediately.
- The Netherlands' CCyB was at 0%, so it had nothing to release. Instead DNB cut the non-releasable systemic buffer of ING, Rabobank and ABN AMRO (3% → 2.5/2/1.5%), and pre-announced a 2% CCyB to replace it (DNB, 17 Mar 2020).
- It also postponed the mortgage risk-weight floor (to 1 Jan 2022); the ECB allowed banks to use P2G and the CCoB.
- Banks were reluctant to use their other buffers because of MDA restrictions and stigma (Couaillier et al. 2022).

**Slide 7 · Paradigm shift: the positive neutral CCyB.**
- BCBS (2024): 17+ jurisdictions have adopted one; the Netherlands, Sweden and the UK set it at 2%.
- Calibration methods include stress tests, historical losses and models (ECB losses-to-buffer approach: 1.1–1.8%, De Nora et al. 2025).
- Transition to the Netherlands.

## Part II: Netherlands deep dive

**Slide 8 · Timeline.**
- 0% from 2016 to 2021.
- 17 March 2020: systemic-buffer cut, with a 2% CCyB pre-announced as the replacement.
- 29 December 2020: SRB converted to O-SII buffers (CRD V).
- 1 January 2022: mortgage risk-weight floor in force.
- February 2022: new framework published.
- 25 May 2022: 1% announced.
- 31 May 2023: 2% announced; June 2023: O-SII cuts announced (effective 31 May 2024, the same day the 2% CCyB became binding).
- Maintained at 2% through September 2026.

**Slide 9 · Economic and financial environment at the decisions.**
- Recovery from COVID (GDP back above its pre-COVID level in 2021Q3); HICP inflation 11.6% in 2022; ECB hikes from July 2022.
- House prices +19% y/y in 2022Q1, then falling.
- Banks' CET1 ratio 17.7%; net interest income +6.7% in 2022.

**Slide 10 · DNB's framework.**
- Four phases.
- Calibration of the 2%: peak accumulated losses of €12bn → 2% ≈ €6bn of releasable capital → supports up to about €150bn of lending.
- Guided discretion rather than a mechanical rule.

**Slide 11 · Criteria DNB itself cited.**
- 2022: risks "normal to elevated"; the economy had recovered from COVID.
- 2023:
  - The credit gap showed no excessive credit growth.
  - House prices were falling.
  - Concerns about risk appetite and debt sustainability.

**Slide 12 · The puzzle in numbers.** All four gap measures map to a 0% CCyB at both decision dates.

**Slide 13 · Other indicators: cold credit, hot housing (Fig. 2).**
- Household debt/GDP is 107%. That is among the highest internationally, about twice the euro area level.
- The ratio fell only because nominal GDP grew faster; the stock of household debt actually rose 12%.
- Debt-service ratio is low.
- Real house prices are 16% above their pre-GFC peak.

**Slide 14 · Core test: normalisation vs overheating.**
- All four predictions point to normalisation (see findings §5).
- Address the strongest counter-evidence directly: house prices at +19% y/y.

**Slide 15 · Which risks the CCyB addresses: assigning tools to risks.**
- CCyB → unforeseeable external shocks. The Netherlands is a small open economy with a banking sector of about 280% of GDP.
- Household debt and housing → borrower-based measures and the mortgage risk-weight floor.
- Systemic importance → the O-SII buffer.
- The O-SII cuts mean capital partly moved from a non-releasable layer to a releasable one: about €2.7bn of O-SII relief vs +€3.4bn CCyB in 2023, so the net change was about +€0.7bn (own calculation; DNB: "limited increase").

**Slide 16 · Implication 1: low cost for banks.**
- €6.7bn compared with the four large banks' CET1 headroom over the MDA (€42bn at end-2021, €36bn at end-2022): ≈8% / ≈19% (upper bounds). The old 6–47% grid goes to the appendix as a sensitivity check.
- Banks' profits benefited from higher rates.

**Slide 17 · Implication 2: no contraction in lending.**
- Bank credit grew +16.6% in the Netherlands vs +7.9% in the euro area (2022Q1–2026Q1).
- Event study (Fig. 3): lending rates moved with euro-area peers (inside the placebo band); household credit growth was higher; nothing happens at May-22, May-23 or May-24.
- Spell out the identification limits.

**Slide 18 · Implication 3: releasable capital.**
- €0 in 2020 vs about €6.7bn now, roughly half of the post-GFC cumulative losses.
- Growth-at-Risk lens: the buffer improves the lower tail in bad states (Lang & Menno 2023).

**Slide 19 · Implication 4: wider effects.**
- Cross-border reciprocity.
- European convergence: France 1%, Germany 0.75%, plus Portugal and Spain.
- Macroprudential policy is moving from rules to judgement.

**Slide 20 · Critiques and open questions.**
- Discretion depends on communication and credibility.
- The 2% calibration is somewhat arbitrary.
- No release has ever happened, so whether banks will actually use the capital is unknown.

- Closing line: *The best time to fill a buffer is when nobody thinks you need it.*
